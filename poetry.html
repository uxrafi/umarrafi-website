Treasures of Time

The artist

Syed Sadequain Ahmed Naqvi (1930–1987), known simply as Sadequain, was born in Amroha, in present-day Uttar Pradesh, into a family of calligraphers. He moved to Karachi after Partition and, largely self-taught, became one of Pakistan’s most celebrated painters. He transformed calligraphy into a living modern art form, won the Paris Biennale in 1961, and received all four of Pakistan’s highest civilian honours.

Sadequain believed art belonged to the public. He rarely sold his work, called himself an “artist of the gutter”, and painted more than thirty-five monumental murals for banks, museums, universities and other public buildings across Pakistan and India, many of them given as gifts.

Why he painted it

Treasures of Time was his first large mural, painted in a burst of work between August and October 1961 for the State Bank of Pakistan in Karachi. Rather than a national or allegorical theme, Sadequain chose a universal one: a celebration of the intellectual achievements of humankind, honouring thinkers from many civilisations side by side. It marked a turning point in his own work, opening the way to the literary and classical subjects of his later paintings.

What it depicts

Around eight feet high and some sixty feet long, the mural traces the history of human thought from ancient Greece to the twentieth century, moving from left to right through 46 figures. It opens with a prehistoric figure drawing a circle, the first act of human thought, and ends with Einstein standing alone beside a clock. In between come philosophers, scientists, poets and writers from Socrates, Plato and Ibn Sina to Galileo, Newton, Shakespeare, Ghalib and Iqbal, with the largest group drawn from the Islamic world. Each is identified by the tools or symbols of their calling: ruined temples and pyramids, the Leaning Tower of Pisa, an astrolabe and a lute, a blazing sun. Rather than literal portraits, Sadequain gives them elongated forms and his sweeping calligraphic line. Near the centre he painted himself among them, a frail figure taking his own pulse before the physician al-Razi. As one writer in Dawn put it, “walking its length feels like walking through centuries.”

The mural first hung in the State Bank’s library. After restoration in 2011, it was installed in the Sadequain Gallery of the State Bank Museum.

Understanding the numbers

The six numbers mark the mural’s broad sections: the Greeks, medieval and Renaissance Italy, the Islamic world, the Enlightenment, South Asia, and Einstein. They are this page’s navigation guide, not part of the mural, and the boundaries are approximate. Individual faces are not labelled; the names given are those cited in published accounts. Bayt al-Hikma

Bayt al-Hikma, the House of Wisdom, was an Abbasid royal library in Baghdad, closely associated with the reign of al-Ma’mun in the ninth century. It belonged to an age of remarkable intellectual exchange, when scholars translated and studied works of philosophy, mathematics, astronomy and medicine from earlier traditions. Across Baghdad’s wider scholarly community, knowledge travelled between languages and cultures, preserving inherited ideas while opening them to new questions. That willingness to learn across boundaries inspires this page.

My Bayt al-Hikma is a modest meeting place for poetry, translations and reflections. Most of the poems gathered here are my English translations of Urdu and my native Punjabi, bringing the voices, ideas and emotions of poets I admire to readers in another language. Alongside them are my own poems and writings, shaped by the countries, friendships and perspectives that have formed me. My hope is to carry something of each voice across languages, inviting understanding and connection through the words we share.

Several of the translations were written under the pen name Bulleya.

English

When death arrives, I shall not die Like a flowing river, I will pass it by A shining donut in a palatial cafe I will simply dissolve into a cup of latte Fear of doomsday; all delusions and dreams Arafat; I will avert, when I hear such screams My heart believes; my mind denies The mosque at a distance; will a temple suffice? God's love heals; the paramour's may churn In my beloveds' arms; in hell I shall burn Satan's debate; the pretensions of faith Walk past the mosque with a quite gait Fate of inquiry; an exit from heavens? Flee such gardens; the Edens and Adns Pursuit is my mosque; this world is my lair Much to be done, beyond worship and prayer Save I behold, Moses' surprise  Convictions of science, I cannot deny Your horde is for pagans and heathens Umar For angels and seers, look somewhere else
Pity the NationUmar Rafi · EnglishPity the nation whose soldier is a businessmanRead poem ↓
Pity the nation whose soldier is a businessman Whose businessman is a politician; whose politician is an actor Whose actor is a priest and whose priest is a soldier Pity the nation whose history is trapped in fiction Whose fiction trapped in religion And whose religion trapped in the minds of those who understand neither fiction nor history Pity the nation where labor is abundant Each child a laborer Each woman a commodity; designated to produce more labor Pity the nation, where drones are cherished But life is cheap Where damage is incidental and death is collateral Pity the nation where liquor flows freely Yet free-thought is a luxury Whose boozers too fake to think; whose thinkers too rigid to drink Pity the nation where only men can dance Its girls bullied into modesty; save for when flesh is on sale Each strut of her body under the shadows of sleazy merriment and ill-begotten currency.... Pity the nation whose canons are loud Whose poets quiet Their poetry filled, not with love and romance, but with revenge and anger Pity the nation whose artists are fearful Whose preachers fearless And whose greatest minds, numb with cynicism, neither fearful nor fearless Pity the nation whose general stands proudly Not at the helm of charging brigades But at the dais of political gatherings....

بحثِ غشاشUmar Rafi · Urdu poetryKoun kehta hae ke mout aaye to mar jaoon gaRead poem ↓
UrduRoman Urdu

کون کہتا ہے کہ موت آئے تو مر جاؤں گا
میں تو دریا ہوں، سمندر میں اتر جاؤں گا

Koun kehta hae ke mout aaye to mar jaoon ga
Mein to daryaa houn, sumandar mein utar jaoon ga

قصرِ ڈنکن کے چمکتے ہوئے ڈونٹ کی طرح
ایک لاٹے کی پیالی میں پگھل جاؤں گا

Qasr-e-Dunkin ke chamktay huway donut ki tarah
Aik latte ki piyali mein pighal jaoon ga

شورِ محشر ہے فقط وہمِ اذیت کی صدا
جبلِ رحمہ کے کنارے سے ہی مڑ جاؤں گا

Shour-e-mehshar hay faqat wahm-e-izziyyat ki sada
Jabl-e-Rahma kay kinaray sae hi murh jaoon ga

دل تو مومن ہے، مگر عقل ابھی کافر ہے
کعبہ ہو دور تو مندر میں چلا جاؤں گا

Dil tou momin hae, magar aqal abhi kaafir hae
Kaaba ho dour tou mandir mein chalaa jaoon ga

عشقِ خالق میں جزا، عشقِ مجازی میں سزا
نظرِ محبوب میں دوزخ سے گزر جاؤں گا

Ishq-e-Khaliq mein jazaa, Ishq-e-majaazi mein sazaa
Nazr-e-mehboob maen dozakh sae guzar jaoon ga

بحثِ ابلیس کہاں، حجتِ ایمان کہاں
دیرِ مسجد سے دبے پاؤں پلٹ آؤں گا

Bahs-e-Iblees kahaan, hujaat-e-imaan kahaan
Dair-e-masjid sae dabay paoon palat aaoon ga

کیا ہے انجامِ طلب؟ خارجِ گلزارِ عدن
رت سے پہلے ہی میں جنت سے نکل جاؤں گا

Kyaa hae anjaam-e-talab?; khaaraj-e-gulzaar-e-Adn
Rut sae pehlay he mein jannat sae nikal jaoon ga

اور بھی کام ہیں عالم میں، عبادت ہی نہیں
زیرِ ابراز میں سجدے میں نہیں جاؤں گا

Aur bhi kaam hein aalam mein, ibaadat he nahin
Zair-e-abraaz mein sajday mein nahin jaoon ga

اے خدا مجھ کو دکھا معجزِ جلوۂ طور
ورنہ میں شاخِ یقینی سے پھسل جاؤں گا

Aye Khuda mujh ko dikha mujaz-e-jalwa-e-toor
Warna mein shaakh-e-yaqeeni sae phisaal jaoon ga

تو تو کچھ اور ہی لگنے لگا غشاش عمر
تیری صحبت میں الٰہی کو کدھر پاؤں گا

Tu tou kuch aur he lagnay lagaa ghushshash Umar
Teri suhbat mein Ilahi ko kidhar paoon ga
