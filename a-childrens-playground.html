Meet the characters

Connections I see between the characters and the wider world. Their lives invite readings beyond my interpretation.

Asad

Asad is the protagonist of A Children’s Playground, which unfolds through his thoughts and life’s journey. In a sense, his journey reflects my own. In fact, in the first draft, he was named Umar. Through him, I explore why otherwise well-meaning people find themselves on opposing sides of national and cultural divides, and why understanding one another does not always bring us together.

As an allegorical figure, he represents a hopeful future for Pakistan, my country of birth. He possesses empathy, intellect, vision and worldly experience, yet he is held back by doubt, indecision and a tendency to reflect when action is needed.

He mirrors a country rich in possibility, yet unable to find its direction—tragedy following tragedy without changing its course, until even those who believe in its promise begin to lose hope, still waiting for a leader who never seems to arrive. Mashal brings that uncertainty into sharper focus: what does hope ask of someone who has the capacity to act?

Anna

Anna is the female protagonist of A Children’s Playground. An eternal optimist, filled with empathy, she personifies what is most admirable about the United States: generosity, openness and faith in possibility. A gifted musician and artist, she brings a curiosity and creative life of her own. In the spirit of Segal’s Love Story, one of the inspirations for my novel, she is both brilliant and beautiful.

For Asad, she is the first and only love of his life, bringing warmth and companionship to a life marked by his mother’s early death, the absence of siblings and an aloof father. She reaches a loneliness he has scarcely learned to name.

Anna is also his enduring bond with America. Even after witnessing the devastation brought to his tribal villages by America’s War on Terror, he cannot separate the country from the woman he loves. Through her eyes, he continues to see its promise and humanity, specifically because it remains the country that gave him Anna—the only woman he ever truly loved.

Samina

Samina personifies the suffering of poorer nations and peoples whose lives are shattered by wars they have little power to prevent. Without the resources to resist or rebuild, she draws on an inner resilience, finally entrusting to fate and the Divine what she cannot hold in her own hands.

She does not utter a single word in the novel, yet her silence carries the weight of a life beset by adversaries both at home and beyond her country’s borders, whose workings she cannot understand and whose power she has neither the words to challenge nor the means to resist. Through her, the novel explores the almost unfathomable capacity to endure without complaint when even the freedom to protest lies beyond reach. Yet from her quiet endurance emerge the Mashals of the future: children in whom a battered generation’s unfinished hopes find another chance to live.

Rahman Baba

Rahman Baba is the moral compass of A Children’s Playground. His wisdom, compassion and unyielding sense of honour give him an authority that wealth and position cannot confer.

As an allegorical figure, he represents the Pashtun heart of Afghan civilisation: its fierce independence, hospitality, loyalty and attachment to land and community. In him, I see a people whose inherited ways of life have been battered by war after war across half a century, yet whose dignity survives the destruction around them.

He longs for change, but lacks the resources, influence and worldly tools to bring it about. His pride will not let him retreat from defending his people; his circumstances deny him the means to prevail. His resilience sustains him, yet the same code of honour can bind him to struggles that offer no clear way forward.

Aunty Ghazala

Aunty Ghazala personifies a small but enduring conscience across the third world, rooted in Pakistan yet familiar far beyond it. Among educated and privileged elites too often accustomed to taking from society, she belongs to the few who feel compelled to give back. She has the means to leave, yet stays out of compassion, responsibility and faith in her country’s promise, working to hold society together against the odds.

Yet education, wealth and determination offer limited power against failures at home and a global order in which influence is so unequally distributed. Like Rahman Baba, though for different reasons, she struggles to turn moral conviction into the means to confront those greater powers, unable to bring about the future she can so clearly imagine.

Shuja Muhkamzai

Shuja Muhkamzai, Asad’s father, embodies the old order in Pakistan’s Pashtun society, with echoes wherever inherited authority struggles to make room for change. He tried to reform the system from within, yet remained bound to its hierarchies and habits. He possessed courage and the capacity to lead, but lacked the compassion and flexibility to become the leader his people needed. The authority that might have enabled change became something he felt compelled to preserve.

Status-conscious and aloof, he struggles to reach the son he loves. His affection remains guarded by the same pride that keeps others at a distance. Too rigid to bend his authority towards the needs of others, and too proud to let anyone question it, he embodies a generation whose desire for change could not overcome its attachment to control.

Col. Mark Manning

Colonel Mark Manning embodies a powerful America, yet increasingly uncertain of its direction as one generation’s convictions give way to another’s questions. His profession carries him from war to war, but the human cost remains distant: something he has not learned to see, or has chosen not to examine. His training prepares him to act, while his limited exposure to the societies affected leaves him ill-equipped to understand what those actions mean to the people who live there.

He is also a compassionate father and a decent man, whose tenderness at home has not found its way to the people whose lives are torn apart by the wars he serves in. He is capable of listening, open to unfamiliar ideas when someone gives them a human voice—even someone from beyond America. He knows distant regions chiefly through conflict, and struggles to recognise in their people the same hopes and fears he understands in his own family.

Mashal

Mashal personifies the future of nations and peoples scarred by conflict, still capable of renewal. The lives of the other characters converge in her: their loves, losses and struggles become the inheritance from which a new generation must build. Though they occupy more of the novel’s pages, she carries its deepest promise—the possibility that their unfulfilled aspirations might finally take root.

Marked by loss and conflict, she stands on the fragile boundary between extreme poverty and extraordinary privilege. Like impoverished nations rich in possibility, she holds a promise that hardship has not extinguished, yet her future rests on choices others make on her behalf.

She embodies the one quality that keeps people and nations striving to survive when reason offers little assurance: hope. She cannot undo what has been destroyed, but in her, the future asks something of everyone who claims to care about it.

The Kite Runner

This book has had the greatest influence on my novel. The boy raised in affluence in a third-world country, mistaking privilege for a birthright and the quiet service of others for something he is owed, follows an all-too-familiar path: kindness received without thought, loyalty taken for granted, and small cruelties whose weight is understood only years later. It is a story I recognise in my own childhood, and one in which I played my part. Empathy arrives late; guilt follows close behind.

In this part of his life, Asad shares the burden of Amir, the privileged boy at the heart of The Kite Runner: growing into the man who must answer for the boy he once was.

The Kite Runner won the 2003 Borders Original Voices Award and has sold more than eight million copies worldwide. I consider Khaled Hosseini, a fellow Californian, the finest writer to emerge from the South Asian and Central Asian region.

The Reluctant Fundamentalist

Published in 2007, this international bestseller spoke to a world still grappling with the aftermath of 9/11. For me, it remains a point of reference for hearing “the other side of the story”: how America’s response to the attacks looked and felt to a young immigrant who had embraced the American dream.

Asad shares some of the questions of identity and belonging that trouble Changez, the young immigrant protagonist of The Reluctant Fundamentalist. These questions helped shape Asad’s inner journey, but his response grows from reflection, compassion and the wish to reconcile the two worlds he calls home.

Asad is less rigid in his convictions. His story unfolds across a lifetime, rather than through a conversation over coffee. Above all, he is a man in love. Anna makes every question of allegiance deeply personal: the world he struggles to understand is also the world in which he has found love. His choices emerge from that tension between love, conscience and duty.

An Equal Music

I consider An Equal Music one of the finest works of contemporary English literature, and Vikram Seth one of the finest authors writing in English today.

Its power comes from the intimacy of its world. It needs no great historical upheaval or mystery to solve: the lives of four musicians, their rehearsals, friendships and tensions, are enough to hold the reader. Running through that world is a lingering love story between Michael, a violinist in the Maggiore Quartet, and Julia, a pianist he loved and lost, who re-enters his life years later.

Julia was an inspiration for Anna in my novel. Both share a deep devotion to music, tested by the boundaries life places around it. Researching Anna’s musical world led me into classical music, symphonies and the lives of composers and performers. What began as research for a character became a discovery of music itself, a journey inspired by An Equal Music.

Love Story

How can any English-language novel about a young man and woman falling in love on an American college campus escape the influence of Love Story? For me, Erich Segal’s enduring classic is the gold standard for such novels, in America and far beyond it. In fact, the first Urdu film I ever saw was Mera Naam Hai Mohabbat (My Name Is Love), a Pakistani adaptation of Love Story.

Asad and Anna are not modelled on Oliver and Jenny, but I wrote their interactions with Segal’s couple always in mind. Jenny, herself a musician, brings wit and an indifference to Oliver’s privilege that give their relationship much of its warmth and life. Like Oliver, Asad finds that love challenges the future his family has imagined for him, asking him to discover who he is beyond the expectations he has inherited.

Most of all, the opening has stayed with me: “What can you say about a twenty-five year old girl who died? That she was beautiful and brilliant.” Alongside those words, the film’s image of Oliver sitting alone on a bench in Central Park inspired the beginning of my novel: a middle-aged Asad, seated on a similar bench at a hill resort in northern Pakistan, looking back on a life lived across two worlds, on the years he spent in California, and on the only woman he ever truly loved—Anna.

ABOUT THE NOVEL

A life divided.
A heart caught between.

A life across cultures, against the backdrop of the Global War on Terror.

A Children’s Playground is a coming-of-age novel set against the backdrop of the Global War on Terror, exploring how love, loss, duty, and war shape the moral journey of one young man. Spanning continents, cultures and religions, it examines the timeless themes of class, family, faith, identity, and fate, asking what it truly means to become the person one is destined to be.

Born into a prominent tribal family along the Pakistan–Afghanistan border, Asad dreams of escaping the expectations of his lineage to build a life in Silicon Valley rather than succeeding his aging father as tribal chief. His carefully planned future is transformed when he meets Anna, a gifted musician and artist at Stanford University whose optimism, curiosity, and free spirit challenge everything he believes about love, happiness, and himself.

As their relationship deepens, the war reaches into the lives of people Asad knows and loves. Its human cost brings the two worlds he calls home into painful collision. Torn between the life he longs to build in America and the responsibilities he has inherited in Pakistan, he must confront what compassion demands when understanding alone is no longer enough.

Stretching from the tribal mountains of northwestern Pakistan to the heart of Silicon Valley, A Children’s Playground is ultimately the story of a boy becoming a man—discovering that the greatest battles are often fought not between nations, but within the human heart, where love and duty, ambition and sacrifice, hope and grief struggle to coexist.

AUTHOR’S NOTE

A city once seen.
A city later spared.

How places, friendships and a life across cultures shaped the novel.

In 1945, U.S. Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson urged that Kyoto be removed from the atomic-bomb target list. His stated concerns included the city’s cultural importance and the lasting bitterness its destruction could leave behind. He had visited Kyoto before the war. Whatever part his memories played, I find something deeply human in the image of a place once encountered and later defended—a city that had become more than a name on a map.

My own life has been an accidental journey through many such Kyotos.

Two decades ago, I set out to write A Children’s Playground. Over those years, my adult life took me through seven countries: Pakistan, my country of birth; Canada, my country of citizenship; the United States, my country of residence; the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Lebanon and Malaysia. These were not simply places I visited, but worlds in which I lived, worked and formed friendships.

I also lived in Iraq as a child for a year and a half, with recollections of the modern city of Baghdad preserved through my parents’ photographs and stories. My father, in his thirties, loved to connect history with objects and lands. Our family frequently journeyed to the sacred city of Karbala, and we explored the historic city of Samarra, sadly marred by destruction during the recent Iraq War.

Seventeen years later, the United States Air Force Academy granted me a distinctive scholarship, an honor bestowed upon only ten foreign students annually. This remarkable opportunity afforded me an inside view of the U.S. military. I flew and jumped out of airplanes with future U.S. Air Force officers, forming lifelong friendships. Among my classmates, I now count individuals who held command positions within the U.S. military during the Afghan, Iraq and Iran wars.

I had the opportunity to share my college living space with a Jewish roommate who was regularly engaged in his religious community's activities, providing me with unique insights into his faith. While pursuing a master's degree, I had an Indian-American Hindu graduate advisor in his thirties. Our academic interactions were frequently laced with conversations weaving the threads of our common Punjabi heritage. For my Ph.D. dissertation, I was guided by a septuagenarian Shia Muslim Iranian-American professor who was a globally renowned researcher in finance and a recognized Islamic scholar.

Interestingly, after completing my initial degree in the USA at twenty-three, I stood at the precipice of adopting atheism.  Twenty-five years later, back into the fold of religion, I authored a book on Islamic finance.

As a child, I rode in my father's car as our family embarked on a road trip that took us from Najaf–the burial place of Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib– in Iraq to a slum city in Kuwait, to vibrant Tehran and onward to a lovely inn in Kabul (with a lot of colorful music and food). We continued our adventure through the historic Khyber Pass, traversing the entire length of Pakistan until we reached Karachi (with memories once again preserved through my parents’ photographs and stories).

A decade later, I found myself in the backseat of a car, much like Asad in A Children’s Playground years later, “captivated by the world passing by, staring outside with my nose plastered against the car window,” as we drove from Orlando to New York, from Disney World to the Statue of Liberty.

During my teenage years, I regularly travelled to remote regions of Pakistan, through villages shaped by long histories, along broken roads in crowded buses, with passengers sometimes riding on the roof.

Along the way, I visited numerous Hindu temples in India, from Jaipur to Ooty. I spent an entire day at the Darbar Sahib Gurdwara in Amritsar, serving food in the langar (communal kitchen), walking into the Golden Temple, listening to verses from Guru Granth Sahib, and trying to understand a religion deeply connected to my native language.

Two weeks later, in Delhi, I poured my heart into translating Bazeecha-e-Atfal (A Children’s Playground), the poem that gives my novel its title, from its original Urdu into English while sitting inside the historic residence of my favorite poet, Mirza Asadullah Khan Ghalib.

My father and five uncles fought in the 1965 and 1971 India-Pakistan wars. My father found himself launching air raids on airfields in cities where he had spent his pre-Partition teenage years a mere eighteen years earlier. Until the end of his life, he wanted to visit those cities to rekindle his childhood memories, yet he could not do so due to strict visa restrictions. Ironically, his only post-Partition visits to those cities were limited to the cockpit of his bomber airplane during the two wars.

Growing up in Islamabad, I observed the Soviet-Afghan War unfold before my home (with the playground that would inspire A Children’s Playground as the backdrop). I saw injured and orphaned, poor Afghan refugee children housed in a newly constructed neighboring madrassah (across the street from the house with the big Alsatian). Decades later, as an adult, I observed the NATO-Afghan War from that very same playground, watching the ordeal of the next generation of injured and orphaned, poor Afghan refugee children. This time, the misery was augmented with injured and orphaned Pakistani children (children like Samina in A Children’s Playground).

That playground, and the generations of children who passed through its world in the shadow of different wars, eventually gave A Children’s Playground its name.

I was in Riyadh during the Arab Spring and Beirut during Lebanese-Israeli skirmishes.

Across these experiences, I kept encountering kindness that crossed religions, races and nationalities, alongside conflicts that set those same communities against one another. Why do compassionate, well-meaning people find themselves on opposing sides of a war? That question became one of the driving forces behind this novel.

I hold lifelong friendships with many individuals symbolically represented through the diverse characters in A Children’s Playground across numerous conflict-ridden borders–Mark Mannings and Saminas, Rahman Babas and Annas, Johans and Fakhars.

Amid the recent Afghan War, some of my American friends carried out bombings, while some of my Afghan friends found themselves as the targets of these same bombings. I struggle to discern substantial distinctions among them. I have eaten Big Macs with the former and drunk chai with the latter throughout my adult life, leading me to firmly believe that if they were all to meet in one place, they would likely appreciate each other's company, just as I cherish their presence, and they would part as friends.

Can knowing one another prevent every war? Perhaps not. But it can make it harder to hate people we have never taken the time to know. Familiarity cannot guarantee compassion; it can give compassion a face, a voice and a place to begin.

I hope the novel makes room for laughter as well as reflection—for the warmth of friendship and the wit of lovers, alongside the questions that trouble them. Above all, I hope it invites readers to look beyond collective loyalties and recognise the individual lives at stake.

If you wield the power of life and death over another person, you owe them the effort of seeing a life as fully as you can. Share a meal, listen to a story, learn something of the world they call home. Such encounters may not resolve a conflict, but they can make it harder to mistake a human being for an abstraction.

In Mashal’s words, “How will you ever do anything if you don’t know anything?”

Friends from countries and communities divided by conflict read early versions of the manuscript. Their perspectives helped me test whether its humanity could be heard across opposing narratives. I write for the optimists among us: those who can recognise another person’s virtues without ignoring their nation’s faults, and who still believe understanding is worth the effort.

To the friends who read and commented on my writing, line by line: any wisdom and insights are yours; all errors and oversights are mine.

MIRZA GHALIB

A poet of Delhi.
A life of loss and verse.

The life of the poet whose ghazal gives the novel its title.

This literary journey takes flight on the wings of Mirza Ghalib’s poetry. To fully comprehend the splendor and intricacy of his verses, one must venture into the world of this fabled poet. For those unfamiliar with his life, a glimpse into his story is the gateway to unlock the treasure trove of his works, and reveal the inspiration behind this book.

Born in Agra, India in 1797, Mirza Asadullah Baig Khan, also known as Mirza Ghalib, was a prolific writer in both Urdu and Persian. Having displayed his genius by writing his first Urdu couplet at the age of 11, Ghalib would go on to write thousands of couplets in Persian and some 1,800 in Urdu. However, it was his Urdu poetry that garnered him acclaim, earning him the title of the greatest poet of the Urdu language.

Ghalib’s poetry is a reflection of his life, shaped by his encounters with the world around him. He lost his father and then his guardian uncle as a young child, further enduring the pain of losing all seven of his children in infancy, leaving a permanent mark on his soul. Additionally, he witnessed the tumultuous Indian Rebellion of 1857, which was a major uprising against British colonial rule in India.

As violence ravaged his beloved Delhi, he became a witness to the chaos and destruction that tore apart his home. While many fled, Ghalib remained, confined to his house in Ballimaran as friends were killed, neighbourhoods emptied and the Mughal court he had served was swept away. In his diary, Dastanbuy (A Bouquet of Flowers), and in letters to friends, he poured out the anguish and helplessness that consumed him.

He never stopped loving Delhi, even as he watched it fall. In his final years, his health failing and much of his world gone, he lived on among the memories of a city that had been both beautiful and tragic. He died there in February 1869, in his beloved Dilli, his heart’s true home, leaving behind a legacy of poetry that continues to inspire and move us to this day.

Although there may have been moments of happiness and success, particularly in his youth when he gained some recognition for his poetry, it would be accurate to say that much of Ghalib’s life was plagued by unmitigated suffering, misery, and grief. The agony of losing his beloved children, the tumultuous times of the Rebellion of 1857 in Delhi, and the numerous other tragedies he faced in his life seeped into Mirza Ghalib’s poignant poetry, which captured the very essence of human emotions such as the profound ache of love, the piercing agony of pain, and the relentless desolation of longing.

Ghalib questioned received truths and mocked hypocrisy in matters of religion and morals. Instead, he advocated for individuals to explore and acknowledge their own weaknesses and failures, recognizing that these are just as important as their achievements, making his poetry not only beautiful but also intellectually stimulating.

Ghalib’s masterful collection of Urdu poetry, Diwan-e-Ghalib (Ghalib’s Collected Poems), has been a cherished treasure since it was first published in 1841, enchanting readers with its evocative imagery and profound insights into the human experience. To this day, it remains one of the most widely read and revered books in South Asia, a testament to the enduring heritage of this great poet.

Ghalib’s legacy has left an indelible mark upon Urdu literature and culture, and he is considered a national treasure in South Asia.

GHALIB & THE TITLE

A children’s playground.
A world at play.

The poem behind the title, and the interplay of love, uncertainty and power.

Mirza GhalibMy tribute to Ghalib

The novel takes its title from the opening words of Mirza Ghalib’s Bazeecha-e-Atfal: the world seen as a children’s playground. That image holds both the fleeting nature of life and the troubling smallness of the games people play with power, love and one another’s futures.

For me, the playground is also literal: the lawn of my childhood home in Islamabad. Its games, friendships and unspoken hierarchies became a small model of the world Asad would grow up trying to understand.

In the earliest draft, Asad was named Umar. His later name connects him to Ghalib, born Mirza Asadullah Baig Khan, who also wrote under the pen name Asad. The connection reflects the novel’s debt to the poet’s questioning spirit, as well as his language.

The ghazal is a poetic form with Arabic origins, developed further in Persian and Urdu, in which love, separation and longing often speak alongside spiritual and philosophical questions. The image of the wounded gazelle is a poetic association I find resonant here, rather than a literal definition of the form. It offers one way of hearing the vulnerability beneath the novel’s conversations about love and belonging.

The version presented here contains fourteen couplets. My English translation is an attempt to carry something of their beauty and uncertainty into another language; the poem and the story of translating it at Ghalib’s home appear under “Read the poem.”

Had I not dedicated this book to my mother, “for planting, flower by flower, the Children’s Playground,” I would have dedicated it to Mirza Ghalib, for giving my book its name, and for keeping me company in every country I have called home.

THE GHAZAL

Ghalib’s words.
Given a voice.

Naseeruddin Shah and Jagjit Singh

Mirza Ghalib (1988)

A television series by Gulzar

In 1988, India’s national broadcaster, Doordarshan, aired Mirza Ghalib, a television series written and directed by the poet and filmmaker Gulzar. It followed Ghalib through the last decades of Mughal Delhi: his debts and disappointments, his friendships and rivalries, his love of the city and his grief at its fall in 1857. In the title role, the celebrated character actor Naseeruddin Shah gave a performance so complete that, for a generation of viewers, he simply became Ghalib.

The ghazals were set to music and sung by the great Jagjit Singh, and his renditions immortalised Ghalib’s verse once and for all, carrying it far beyond readers of Urdu poetry into homes across South Asia and beyond. Among them is Bazeecha-e-Atfal, the ghazal whose opening words give my novel its title.

RAHMAN BABA

A poet of peace.
A man of honour.

The Sufi poet of Pashto, and the character in the novel who carries his name.

The poet

Abdul Rahman Mohmand, known to millions simply as Rahman Baba, is the most beloved poet of the Pashto language. He lived in the second half of the seventeenth century in the villages on the edge of Peshawar, not far from the Khyber Pass, and little is known of his life for certain. He is remembered instead through his Diwan, a collection of poems still recited in homes, mosques and gatherings across Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Afghanistan.

His poetry belongs to the Sufi tradition. It speaks of love for God and for all people, of humility and generosity, and of the emptiness of wealth and pride. One of his most quoted couplets advises: “Sow flowers, so that your surroundings become a garden; do not sow thorns, for they will prick your own feet.” Another reminds readers that all human beings are one body, and that whoever torments another wounds himself.

His shrine at Hazar Khwani, on the outskirts of Peshawar, has long been a place of pilgrimage, music and poetry. In 2009 it was bombed by militants who objected to the very openness it stood for, an attack that shocked Pashtuns on both sides of the border.

The character

In A Children’s Playground, Rahman Baba is Rahman Khan Muhkamzai, the family’s chauffeur for more than three decades and, to Asad, something far greater. A Pashtun from the villages near Lwarkotal, at the foot of the Khyber Pass, he carries himself with an unmistakable nobility: six feet tall, his salt-and-pepper beard meticulously groomed, washing the family car “as if polishing the Koh-i-Noor diamond.” He has a fifth-grade village education, has never travelled outside Pakistan, and speaks to Asad’s father in a single English word, Sir, “spoken in uncountable tones.”

From the time Asad is five until he is twenty-four, the car becomes their classroom. On the road, away from his parents, Rahman Baba shows the boy the country as it looks from below, quietly arguing against the views of the powerful men he drives, all within an unwritten code that Asad will never repeat what he hears. Among the poor of his own community he is something else again: an elder whose arrival makes women stand and children run to him, “the kind of man who could lead a revolution if he ever put his mind to it.”

He never misses a day and never breaks his word, and despite a lifetime of hardship he lets no bitterness take root. His guiding principle is simple: “If you know you’re morally correct, then don’t worry about the consequences; leave that to Allah.” But he is also a son of Pashtunwali (the Pashtun code of honour), with its hospitality, its honour, its duty to protect family and community, and its law of badal (revenge).

Two Rahman Babas

The novel’s Rahman Baba shares much with the poet whose name he carries: an indifference to wealth, a deep compassion for the poor, a faith that judges deeds by their intentions, and wisdom offered freely to anyone who seeks it. Yet the novel calls him not a Sufi but a tribal warrior. Between the poet’s message of love and the warrior’s code of honour lies one of the book’s central questions: what a good man does when the people he loves are under attack, and what it costs him.

PLACES IN THE NOVEL

From the Margallas.
To Silicon Valley.

The places that shape the story, from a front lawn in Islamabad to a small fort on the Afghan border, a drone base in the Nevada desert and a university in the Bay Area.

The house in Islamabad

The novel takes its title from a front lawn: that of “the last house in Islamabad,” right under the Margalla Hills. A black metal gate opens onto a driveway that slopes down to the house and to a lawn lined on three sides with roses and on the fourth with raat ki rani (night-blooming jasmine), its boundary walls slowly disappearing under ivy. At the back of the house, a hundred feet and a whole world away from the lawn, stand the servant quarters: two cramped rooms facing the rear wall.

In A Children’s Playground, the lawn is a real place and also a small model of the world Asad will spend his life trying to understand. Who bats, who fields, and who chases the ball into the gutter are decided long before the game starts.

The Margalla Hills

The Margalla Hills are the first foothills of the Himalayas, rising in a green wall directly behind Islamabad. Covered in forest and laced with walking trails, they shelter leopards, monkeys and hundreds of species of birds, and the white minarets of the Faisal Mosque stand at their feet.

In the novel, they are the backdrop to Asad’s childhood in “the last house in Islamabad” at their foot.

For me, growing up, they were barely a hundred metres from our gate. I spent my days hiking up and down their trails and cycling along their edge, chasing monkeys and coming home with scraped knees. Most of all, I remember walking beside them with my late father every evening before dinner, on the path to the Faisal Mosque and back, as the light faded on the hills and we talked of history, poetry, war, music and what made life worth living.

Data Darbar, Lahore

Data Darbar (the Court of the Giver) is the shrine of Ali Hujwiri, the eleventh-century Persian Sufi saint known as Data Ganj Bakhsh (Bestower of Treasures), whose Kashf al-Mahjub (The Unveiling of the Veiled) is one of the earliest works on Sufism in Persian. Set beside the walled city of Lahore, it is one of the largest and most visited shrines in South Asia. Day and night, pilgrims of every class pray in its marble courtyards, and its langar, the free communal kitchen, feeds thousands of people every day.

In the novel, it is where Asad’s mother spends thirty days and nights in prayer before he is born, a place of faith that runs quietly beneath his story.

The Khyber Pass

For more than two thousand years, the Khyber Pass has been one of the great gateways of the world. Winding through the Spin Ghar mountains between Peshawar and the Afghan border, it carried traders, pilgrims, poets and armies between Central Asia and the subcontinent, and every empire that wanted India had to pass this way.

At its western end lies Torkham, the busiest crossing on the Durand Line, the border drawn by the British in 1893 that divides the Pashtun lands, and in the novel the Muhkamzai tribe, between Pakistan and Afghanistan. During the war in Afghanistan, the road through the pass was also the main supply route for NATO forces.

In the novel, the road through the Khyber leads Asad back to the land of his ancestors, and to the world that shaped Rahman Baba.

The tribal areas and Lwarkotal

Along the Afghan border lay the Tribal Areas, formerly known as FATA (Federally Administered Tribal Areas): a rugged belt of mountains and valleys home to Pashtun tribes who had governed themselves for centuries under their own code, Pashtunwali. Under a colonial-era system that survived until their merger with Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in 2018, they stood largely outside Pakistan’s ordinary laws, and during the War on Terror they became the front line of military operations and drone strikes.

The novel’s Lwarkotal is fictional: a small town at the foot of the Khyber Pass, three miles from the border, where the Shalmani, Shinwari, Afridi, Mulagori and Muhkamzai tribes meet. To Asad, it is “the land of my warrior ancestors.” Its landscape is modelled on Tari Mangal, pictured above.

The ancestral haveli

Outside Lwarkotal stands the ancestral home of Asad’s family, more fortress than house: eight-foot walls, a courtyard larger than the whole of the house in Islamabad, and the watchful architecture of a land where every family has had to defend itself. Compounds like it, built of mud and stone with corner towers, are still the homes of many families along the frontier.

Between the lawn in Islamabad and the walls of the haveli lie the two inheritances Asad carries: one of comfort and privilege, the other of tribe, honour and duty.

Creech Air Force Base and the Mojave Desert

Creech Air Force Base lies in the Mojave Desert, about 35 miles northwest of Las Vegas, near the small town of Indian Springs. It is the home of the U.S. Air Force’s remotely piloted aircraft, and from its ground control stations pilots fly drones over battlefields on the other side of the world.

In the novel, the base’s gray rock and barren ridges look “more like Afghanistan’s Dasht-e-Margo than an American military installation,” and the villages on the drone pilots’ monitors look like the land outside the gate: a desert in Nevada that mirrors the frontier.

Stanford University

Founded in 1885 by Leland and Jane Stanford in memory of their only son, Stanford University sits among the sandstone arcades and palm-lined avenues of Palo Alto, in the heart of Silicon Valley. Its students and teachers helped build Silicon Valley itself, from Hewlett-Packard to Google, and it remains one of the world’s great centres of learning and invention.

In the novel, Stanford stands at the far end of Asad’s journey: a world of ideas and ambition half a world away from the Khyber. Most importantly, it is where, in a chance encounter at Dinkelspiel Auditorium, Anna, a gifted musician, enters his life and changes him forever.

Hunza Valley — where the story begins

The story begins at a quiet retreat in Hunza Valley, beneath the Karakoram peaks. Seated on a bench above a small lake, Asad looks back on a life lived across two worlds.
