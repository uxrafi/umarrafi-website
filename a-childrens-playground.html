ABOUT THE NOVEL

A life divided.
A heart caught between.

An alternate contemporary setting, against the backdrop of the Global War on Terror.

A Children’s Playground is a coming-of-age novel set against the backdrop of the Global War on Terror, exploring how love, loss, duty, and war shape the moral journey of one young man. Spanning continents, cultures, and religions in an alternate contemporary setting, it examines the timeless themes of class, family, faith, identity, and fate, asking what it truly means to become the person one is destined to be.

Born into a prominent tribal family along the Pakistan–Afghanistan border, Asad dreams of escaping the expectations of his lineage to build a life in Silicon Valley rather than succeeding his aging father as tribal chief. His carefully planned future is transformed when he meets Anna, a gifted musician and artist at Stanford University whose optimism, curiosity, and free spirit challenge everything he believes about love, happiness, and himself.

As their relationship deepens, tragedy strikes both worlds Asad calls home. A devastating drone strike in his remote mountain village leaves his childhood friend, Samina, critically injured and forces him to confront the human cost of a conflict he has spent years trying to leave behind. Torn between the life he longs to build in America and the responsibilities he has inherited in Pakistan, Asad embarks on a journey that will ultimately cost him the woman he loves—not once, but twice.

Stretching from the tribal mountains of northwestern Pakistan to the heart of Silicon Valley, A Children’s Playground is ultimately the story of a boy becoming a man—discovering that the greatest battles are often fought not between nations, but within the human heart, where love and duty, ambition and sacrifice, hope and grief struggle to coexist.

AUTHOR’S NOTE

A city once seen.
A city later spared.

How Kyoto, and a life lived across seven countries, shaped the novel.

Henry L. Stimson, at the age of seventy-eight, held a position of significant influence within the U.S. government during the height of World War II. Serving as the U.S. Secretary of War under Presidents Roosevelt and Truman, his leadership extended to a project of monumental consequence that would forever reshape the world—the Manhattan Project. As Secretary of War, Stimson exercised enormous influence over the Manhattan Project and the deliberations surrounding the use of the atomic bomb. Stimson was a proponent of using Little Boy as soon as it became feasible.

In mid-1945, a U.S. targeting committee presented Henry L. Stimson with a list of five Japanese cities as potential targets. These cities met specific criteria, including being strategically important within urban areas exceeding three miles in diameter and deemed susceptible to significant damage from a blast.

The initial list of potential target cities included Kyoto, situated in the central part of Honshu Island, followed by Hiroshima, Yokohama, Kokura Arsenal, and Niigata. However, Henry L. Stimson promptly removed Kyoto from consideration as a potential target, offering official reasons that appeared contradictory. In the end, the United States did drop two atomic bombs on Japan, with Hiroshima and Nagasaki being the chosen targets.

Subsequently, insights gleaned from Henry L. Stimson's diaries shed light on his decision to exclude Kyoto as a potential target. His concerns extended beyond the war itself to the lasting bitterness that the destruction of such a city could leave behind. Stimson had visited Kyoto in 1926 and 1929, becoming acquainted with the city and its cultural heritage. As Secretary of War, he repeatedly urged that Kyoto be spared, securing President Truman’s agreement. Whatever part his own memories may have played, I find something deeply human in the image of a place once encountered and later defended—a city that had become more than a name on a map.

My own life has been an accidental journey through many such Kyotos.

Two decades ago, I set out to write A Children’s Playground. Over these twenty years, I ventured on an international journey, which provided me with uncommon life experiences of residing in seven different countries as an adult: Pakistan (my country of birth), Canada (my country of citizenship), the United States (my country of residence), UAE, Saudi Arabia, Lebanon, and Malaysia.

I also lived in Iraq as a child for a year and a half, with recollections of the modern city of Baghdad preserved through my parents’ photographs and stories. My father, in his thirties, loved to connect history with objects and lands. Our family frequently journeyed to the sacred city of Karbala, and we explored the historic city of Samarra, sadly marred by destruction during the recent Iraq War.

Seventeen years later, the United States Air Force Academy granted me a distinctive scholarship, an honor bestowed upon only ten foreign students annually. This remarkable opportunity afforded me an inside view of the U.S. military. I flew and jumped out of airplanes with future U.S. Air Force officers, forming lifelong friendships. Among my classmates, I now count individuals who held command positions within the U.S. military during the Afghan, Iraq and Iran wars.

I had the opportunity to share my college living space with a Jewish roommate who was regularly engaged in his religious community's activities, providing me with unique insights into his faith. While pursuing a master's degree, I had an Indian-American Hindu graduate advisor in his thirties. Our academic interactions were frequently laced with conversations weaving the threads of our common Punjabi heritage. For my Ph.D. dissertation, I was guided by a septuagenarian Shia Muslim Iranian-American professor who was a globally renowned researcher in finance and a recognized Islamic scholar.

Interestingly, after completing my initial degree in the USA at twenty-three, I stood at the precipice of adopting atheism.  Twenty-five years later, back into the fold of religion, I authored a book on Islamic finance.

As a child, I rode in my father's car as our family embarked on a road trip that took us from Najaf–the burial place of Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib– in Iraq to a slum city in Kuwait, to vibrant Tehran and onward to a lovely inn in Kabul (with a lot of colorful music and food). We continued our adventure through the historic Khyber Pass, traversing the entire length of Pakistan until we reached Karachi (with memories once again preserved through my parents’ photographs and stories).

A decade later, I found myself in the backseat of a car, much like Asad in A Children’s Playground years later, “captivated by the world passing by, staring outside with my nose plastered against the car window,” as we drove from Orlando to New York, from Disney World to the Statue of Liberty.

During my teenage years, I regularly ventured to the remotest regions of Pakistan through pre-B.C. era villages, navigating broken roads in buses packed to capacity with the poorer passengers spilling over onto the roof.

Along the way, I visited numerous Hindu temples in India, from Jaipur to Ooty. I spent an entire day at the Darbar Sahib Gurdwara in Amritsar, serving food in the langar, walking into the Golden Temple, listening to verses from Guru Granth Sahib, and trying to understand a religion deeply connected to my native language.

Two weeks later, in Delhi, I poured my heart into translating Bazeecha-e-Atfal (A Children’s Playground), the poem that gives my novel its title, from its original Urdu into English while sitting inside the historic residence of my favorite poet, Mirza Asadullah Khan Ghalib.

My father and five uncles fought in the 1965 and 1971 India-Pakistan wars. My father found himself launching air raids on airfields in cities where he had spent his pre-Partition teenage years a mere eighteen years earlier. Until the end of his life, he wanted to visit those cities to rekindle his childhood memories, yet he could not do so due to strict visa restrictions. Ironically, his only post-Partition visits to those cities were limited to the cockpit of his bomber airplane during the two wars.

Growing up in Islamabad, I observed the Soviet-Afghan War unfold before my home (with the playground that would inspire A Children’s Playground as the backdrop). I saw injured and orphaned, poor Afghan refugee children housed in a newly constructed neighboring madrassah (across the street from the house with the big Alsatian). Decades later, as an adult, I observed the NATO-Afghan War from that very same playground, watching the ordeal of the next generation of injured and orphaned, poor Afghan refugee children. This time, the misery was augmented with injured and orphaned Pakistani children (children like Samina in A Children’s Playground).

That playground, and the generations of children who passed through its world in the shadow of different wars, eventually gave A Children’s Playground its name.

I was in Riyadh during the Arab Spring and Beirut during Lebanese-Israeli skirmishes.

Almost every nation I have referred to has, at some point during my lifetime, been involved in conflicts, whether against each other or in alliance, leading to the tragic loss of millions of human lives.

Amidst these varied experiences, one overarching observation became clear: there is a pervasive kindness among individuals transcending religions, races, and nationalities. However, it is inevitably overwhelmed by the collective conflicts that arise between these very religions, races, and nationalities.

This raised a profound question in my mind, and I have been on a lifelong quest to find its answer: Why do compassionate, well-meaning people across the globe perpetually find themselves ensnared in the turmoil of wars?

This question is one of the driving forces behind my writing. Across my books and other writings, I return to questions of uncertainty, competing perspectives and the ways people and systems respond to change.

Can we find a way to prevent all these wars?

Perhaps not. But perhaps we can make it a little harder to hate those we have never taken the time to know.

My encounters with friends and adversaries have left me with a hope: that when we come to know our own Kyotos, the lives within them become harder to dismiss. Familiarity cannot guarantee compassion, but it can give compassion a face, a voice, a place to begin.

I hold lifelong friendships with many individuals symbolically represented through the diverse characters in A Children’s Playground across numerous conflict-ridden borders–Mark Mannings and Saminas, Rahman Babas and Annas, Johans and Fakhars.

Amid the recent Afghan War, some of my American friends carried out bombings, while some of my Afghan friends found themselves as the targets of these same bombings. I struggle to discern substantial distinctions among them. I have eaten Big Macs with the former and drunk chai with the latter throughout my adult life, leading me to firmly believe that if they were all to meet in one place, they would likely appreciate each other's company, just as I cherish their presence, and they would part as friends.

So, what should we do?

In my opinion, the greatest gift one can give someone is to make them laugh and to make them think. I hope my books and writings make you laugh and, more importantly, make you think. I hope they push you out of your comfort zone and challenge your definitions of global rights and wrongs. Most of all, I hope they help you realize that entrusting life-and-death determinations solely to governments and the media can reduce one to a mere pawn manipulated by external forces.

It is straightforward to hold negative sentiments toward individuals one has never encountered and perhaps even simpler to harbor animosity toward places one has never visited. However, it becomes exceptionally challenging to nurture hatred, let alone contemplate the destruction of people and places woven into the fabric of our memories.

If you find yourself in a position where you wield the power of life and death over another person, you owe them the effort of seeing a life as fully as you can. Share a meal, listen to a story, learn something of the world they call home. Such encounters may not resolve every conflict, but they can make it harder to mistake a human being for an abstraction.

In the words of the precocious Mashal in A Children’s Playground, “How will you ever do anything if you don’t know anything?”

When I submitted the manuscript of A Children’s Playground for initial reviews, my intent was to ensure it was concurrently assessed by friends hailing from countries and groups that have been at odds with each other–Americans and Russians, Indians and Pakistanis, Jews and Muslims, Pashtuns and Tajiks, Serbs and Croats, Shias and Sunnis, to mention a few. I aimed to determine whether they could grasp the novel’s core message despite the constant barrage of opposing narratives from their respective media and leaders. Without exception, each one of them understood the essence of the message.

In that sense, my books and writings are for those courageous enough to place their faith in the common human ideal—hope; those who endeavor to see the enduring individual virtues of others, irrespective of collective national faults; those who are ready to forgive, convinced that one day humanity will find its shared North Star even if we repeatedly wander off the path. I write for the optimists among us.

I must thank all my friends who spent time reviewing and commenting on my writing, line by line—a task I have personally found far too tedious to undertake for others. Any wisdom and insights in my books and writings are yours, while all errors and oversights are mine.

Shukriya. Dhanyavaad. Hvala. Spasibo. Mulțumesc. Shukran. Mannana. Toda.
Dhonnobad. Merci. Thank You.

To you, the reader, I offer my sincere gratitude for taking the time to read my books and writings, with the hope that you may embark on your own personal Kyoto adventure.

MIRZA GHALIB

A poet of Delhi.
A life of loss and verse.

The life of the poet whose ghazal gives the novel its title.

This literary journey takes flight on the wings of Mirza Ghalib’s poetry. To fully comprehend the splendor and intricacy of his verses, one must venture into the world of this fabled poet. For those unfamiliar with his life, a glimpse into his story is the gateway to unlock the treasure trove of his works, and reveal the inspiration behind this book.

Born in Agra, India in 1797, Mirza Asadullah Baig Khan, also known as Mirza Ghalib, was a prolific writer in both Urdu and Persian. Having displayed his genius by writing his first Urdu couplet at the age of 11, Ghalib would go on to write thousands of couplets in Persian and some 1,800 in Urdu. However, it was his Urdu poetry that garnered him acclaim, earning him the title of the greatest poet of the Urdu language.

Ghalib’s poetry is a reflection of his life, shaped by his encounters with the world around him. He lost his father and then his guardian uncle as a young child, further enduring the pain of losing all seven of his children in infancy, leaving a permanent mark on his soul. Additionally, he witnessed the tumultuous Indian Rebellion of 1857, which was a major uprising against British colonial rule in India.

As violence ravaged his beloved Delhi, he became a witness to the chaos and destruction that tore apart his home. While many fled, Ghalib remained, confined to his house in Ballimaran as friends were killed, neighbourhoods emptied and the Mughal court he had served was swept away. In his diary, Dastanbuy, and in letters to friends, he poured out the anguish and helplessness that consumed him.

He never stopped loving Delhi, even as he watched it fall. In his final years, his health failing and much of his world gone, he lived on among the memories of a city that had been both beautiful and tragic. He died there in February 1869, in his beloved Dilli, his heart’s true home, leaving behind a legacy of poetry that continues to inspire and move us to this day.

Although there may have been moments of happiness and success, particularly in his youth when he gained some recognition for his poetry, it would be accurate to say that much of Ghalib’s life was plagued by unmitigated suffering, misery, and grief. The agony of losing his beloved children, the tumultuous times of the Rebellion of 1857 in Delhi, and the numerous other tragedies he faced in his life seeped into Mirza Ghalib’s poignant poetry, which captured the very essence of human emotions such as the profound ache of love, the piercing agony of pain, and the relentless desolation of longing.

Ghalib questioned received truths and mocked hypocrisy in matters of religion and morals. Instead, he advocated for individuals to explore and acknowledge their own weaknesses and failures, recognizing that these are just as important as their achievements, making his poetry not only beautiful but also intellectually stimulating.

Ghalib’s masterful collection of Urdu poetry, Diwan-e-Ghalib, has been a cherished treasure since it was first published in 1841, enchanting readers with its evocative imagery and profound insights into the human experience. To this day, it remains one of the most widely read and revered books in South Asia, a testament to the enduring heritage of this great poet.

Ghalib’s legacy has left an indelible mark upon Urdu literature and culture, and he is considered a national treasure in South Asia.

GHALIB & THE TITLE

A children’s playground.
A world at play.

The ghazal behind the title, and the two meanings of “ghazal” that shape its characters.

The title of this novel is derived from the opening phrase of the first verse of one of the most celebrated ghazals by Mirza Ghalib, the nineteenth-century Urdu and Persian poet - Bazeecha-e-Atfal. Translating the non-English dialogue and poetry present in the book into English proved to be a daunting task. However, I recognize the importance of providing readers with an understanding of the final poem that concludes the book. It is challenging to retain the essence of Ghalib's words in any translation. However, I hope my efforts enable readers to appreciate the beauty of his language and the depth of his poetry, even in translation.

The term Bazeecha-e-atfal is derived from two words: Bazeecha which means a playground or play area, and Atfal, which is the plural of the Arabic word tifl meaning child. Therefore, the phrase can be translated to mean ‘A Children's Playground.’ However, the phrase also carries a poetic connotation, evoking the idea of the fleeting nature of life and the impermanence of our existence. In the context of the book, the phrase refers to the front yard of the house in Islamabad where I spent my childhood. The yard is described in detail in the book, providing readers with a vivid sense of the physical space that inspired the title.

The novel’s debt to Ghalib goes beyond its title. Its protagonist, Asad Khan Muhkamzai, is named after the poet himself. Ghalib was not the poet’s real name but his nom de plume, an Arabic word meaning “dominant” or “victorious”; he was born Mirza Asadullah Baig Khan. Asad, drawn from his own name, Asadullah, was also the first pen name he wrote under as a young poet, and it still signs some of his ghazals. So the boy at the heart of the story carries the poet’s own name, and something of his spirit.

Ghazal originated as an Arabic poetic form in the 7th century and spread to Iran and South Asia during the 12th century under the influence of Sufi mystics. Due to its strict restrictions on rhyme and meter, it is one of the most difficult genres of poetry to write and to sing.

The root word of ghazal - ghazl - has multiple meanings, one of which is ‘talking to women.’ Another meaning is the ‘final dying wail of a young graceful doe,’ as it is killed by a hunter's arrow. The English word ‘gazelle,’ is also derived from ghazl. Thus, a ghazal is used to express admiration for women along with the tragedies of unrequited and unfulfilled love that dies a tragic death well before its time.

The dual meaning of the term ghazal provides a nuanced context for the poetic communication between the book's main characters. The first meaning of the term sets the stage for the conversations between Asad and his aunt Ghazala, whose name is also derived from the same term, ghazl. The second meaning of the term adds an extra layer of interpretation to the interactions between the two main characters, Asad and Anna, evoking a palpable, albeit tense, romanticism that culminates in an unrequited and tragic end. By exploring the different facets of this complex term, the book invites readers to consider the nuances of language and communication, and the ways in which meaning can be shaped by context.

This ghazal consists of eleven couplets, each of which explores a different aspect of the human experience. It is said to have been inspired by a conversation that Ghalib had with a group of children, who were playing with a toy birdcage. The children asked Ghalib to recite a poem, and he responded by reciting Bazeecha-e-Atfal for them. The poem is a complex and nuanced exploration of the human condition, and touches on themes such as love, loss, and the fleeting nature of life.

My English translation of Bazeecha-e-Atfal is a modest attempt to bring the beauty of the Urdu language to a wider audience. I completed it in Purani Dilli (Old Delhi), close to the Chandni Chowk marketplace, in the Ballimaran neighborhood on Gali Qasim Jaan street. It was a delightful occasion for me, as I worked on the translation while sitting in the house of the legendary poet himself, which now serves as a small museum.

THE GHAZAL

Ghalib’s words.
Given a voice.

Naseeruddin Shah and Jagjit Singh

Mirza Ghalib (1988)

A television series by Gulzar

In 1988, India’s national broadcaster, Doordarshan, aired Mirza Ghalib, a television series written and directed by the poet and filmmaker Gulzar. It followed Ghalib through the last decades of Mughal Delhi: his debts and disappointments, his friendships and rivalries, his love of the city and his grief at its fall in 1857. In the title role, the celebrated character actor Naseeruddin Shah gave a performance so complete that, for a generation of viewers, he simply became Ghalib.

The ghazals were set to music and sung by the great ghazal singer Jagjit Singh, with Chitra Singh, and his renditions immortalised Ghalib’s verse once and for all, carrying it far beyond readers of Urdu poetry into homes across South Asia and beyond. Among them is Bazeecha-e-Atfal, the ghazal whose opening words give my novel its title.

RAHMAN BABA

A poet of peace.
A man of honour.

The Sufi poet of Pashto, and the character in the novel who carries his name.

The poet

Abdul Rahman Mohmand, known to millions simply as Rahman Baba, is the most beloved poet of the Pashto language. He lived in the second half of the seventeenth century in the villages on the edge of Peshawar, not far from the Khyber Pass, and little is known of his life for certain. He is remembered instead through his Diwan, a collection of poems still recited in homes, mosques and gatherings across Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Afghanistan.

His poetry belongs to the Sufi tradition. It speaks of love for God and for all people, of humility and generosity, and of the emptiness of wealth and pride. One of his most quoted couplets advises: “Sow flowers, so that your surroundings become a garden; do not sow thorns, for they will prick your own feet.” Another reminds readers that all human beings are one body, and that whoever torments another wounds himself.

His shrine at Hazar Khwani, on the outskirts of Peshawar, has long been a place of pilgrimage, music and poetry. In 2009 it was bombed by militants who objected to the very openness it stood for, an attack that shocked Pashtuns on both sides of the border.

The character

In A Children’s Playground, Rahman Baba is Rahman Khan Muhkamzai, the family’s chauffeur for more than three decades and, to Asad, something far greater. A Pashtun from the villages near Lwarkotal, at the foot of the Khyber Pass, he carries himself with an unmistakable nobility: six feet tall, his salt-and-pepper beard meticulously groomed, washing the family car “as if polishing the Koh-i-Noor diamond.” He has a fifth-grade village education, has never travelled outside Pakistan, and speaks to Asad’s father in a single English word, Sir, “spoken in uncountable tones.”

From the time Asad is five until he is twenty-four, the car becomes their classroom. On the road, away from his parents, Rahman Baba shows the boy the country as it looks from below, quietly arguing against the views of the powerful men he drives, all within an unwritten code that Asad will never repeat what he hears. Among the poor of his own community he is something else again: an elder whose arrival makes women stand and children run to him, “the kind of man who could lead a revolution if he ever put his mind to it.”

He never misses a day and never breaks his word, and despite a lifetime of hardship he lets no bitterness take root. His guiding principle is simple: “If you know you’re morally correct, then don’t worry about the consequences; leave that to Allah.” But he is also a son of Pashtunwali, the code of the Pashtuns, with its hospitality, its honour, its duty to protect family and community, and its law of badal, or revenge.

Two Rahman Babas

The novel’s Rahman Baba shares much with the poet whose name he carries: an indifference to wealth, a deep compassion for the poor, a faith that judges deeds by their intentions, and wisdom offered freely to anyone who seeks it. Yet the novel calls him not a Sufi but a tribal warrior. Between the poet’s message of love and the warrior’s code of honour lies one of the book’s central questions: what a good man does when the people he loves are under attack, and what it costs him.

PLACES IN THE NOVEL

From the Margallas.
To Silicon Valley.

The places that shape the story, from a front lawn in Islamabad to a small fort on the Afghan border, a drone base in the Nevada desert and a university in the Bay Area.

The house in Islamabad

The novel takes its title from a front lawn: that of “the last house in Islamabad,” right under the Margalla Hills. A black metal gate opens onto a driveway that slopes down to the house and to a lawn lined on three sides with roses and on the fourth with raat ki rani, the night-blooming jasmine, its boundary walls slowly disappearing under ivy. At the back of the house, a hundred feet and a whole world away from the lawn, stand the servant quarters: two cramped rooms facing the rear wall.

In A Children’s Playground, the lawn is a real place and also a small model of the world Asad will spend his life trying to understand. Who bats, who fields, and who chases the ball into the gutter are decided long before the game starts.

The Margalla Hills

The Margalla Hills are the first foothills of the Himalayas, rising in a green wall directly behind Islamabad. Covered in forest and laced with walking trails, they shelter leopards, monkeys and hundreds of species of birds, and the white minarets of the Faisal Mosque stand at their feet.

In the novel, they are the backdrop to Asad’s childhood in “the last house in Islamabad” at their foot.

For me, growing up, they were barely a hundred metres from our gate. I spent my days hiking up and down their trails and cycling along their edge, chasing monkeys and coming home with scraped knees. Most of all, I remember walking beside them with my late father every evening before dinner, on the path to the Faisal Mosque and back, as the light faded on the hills and we talked of history, poetry, war, music and what made life worth living.

Data Darbar, Lahore

Data Darbar is the shrine of Ali Hujwiri, the eleventh-century Persian Sufi saint known as Data Ganj Bakhsh, the “bestower of treasures,” whose Kashf al-Mahjub is one of the earliest works on Sufism in Persian. Set beside the walled city of Lahore, it is one of the largest and most visited shrines in South Asia. Day and night, pilgrims of every class pray in its marble courtyards, and its langar, the free communal kitchen, feeds thousands of people every day.

In the novel, it is where Asad’s mother spends thirty days and nights in prayer before he is born, a place of faith that runs quietly beneath his story.

The Khyber Pass

For more than two thousand years, the Khyber Pass has been one of the great gateways of the world. Winding through the Spin Ghar mountains between Peshawar and the Afghan border, it carried traders, pilgrims, poets and armies between Central Asia and the subcontinent, and every empire that wanted India had to pass this way.

At its western end lies Torkham, the busiest crossing on the Durand Line, the border drawn by the British in 1893 that divides the Pashtun lands, and in the novel the Muhkamzai tribe, between Pakistan and Afghanistan. During the war in Afghanistan, the road through the pass was also the main supply route for NATO forces.

In the novel, the road through the Khyber leads Asad back to the land of his ancestors, and to the world that shaped Rahman Baba.

The tribal areas and Lwarkotal

Along the Afghan border lay the Tribal Areas, formerly known as FATA (Federally Administered Tribal Areas): a rugged belt of mountains and valleys home to Pashtun tribes who had governed themselves for centuries under their own code, Pashtunwali. Under a colonial-era system that survived until their merger with Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in 2018, they stood largely outside Pakistan’s ordinary laws, and during the War on Terror they became the front line of military operations and drone strikes.

The novel’s Lwarkotal is fictional: a small town at the foot of the Khyber Pass, three miles from the border, where the Shalmani, Shinwari, Afridi, Mulagori and Muhkamzai tribes meet. To Asad, it is “the land of my warrior ancestors.”

The ancestral haveli

Outside Lwarkotal stands the ancestral home of Asad’s family, more fortress than house: eight-foot walls, a courtyard larger than the whole of the house in Islamabad, and the watchful architecture of a land where every family has had to defend itself. Compounds like it, built of mud and stone with corner towers, are still the homes of many families along the frontier.

Between the lawn in Islamabad and the walls of the haveli lie the two inheritances Asad carries: one of comfort and privilege, the other of tribe, honour and duty.

Creech Air Force Base and the Mojave Desert

Creech Air Force Base lies in the Mojave Desert, about 35 miles northwest of Las Vegas, near the small town of Indian Springs. It is the home of the U.S. Air Force’s remotely piloted aircraft, and from its ground control stations pilots fly drones over battlefields on the other side of the world.

In the novel, the base’s gray rock and barren ridges look “more like Afghanistan’s Dasht-e-Margo than an American military installation,” and the villages on the drone pilots’ monitors look like the land outside the gate: a desert in Nevada that mirrors the frontier.

Stanford University

Founded in 1885 by Leland and Jane Stanford in memory of their only son, Stanford University sits among the sandstone arcades and palm-lined avenues of Palo Alto, in the heart of Silicon Valley. Its students and teachers helped build the Valley itself, from Hewlett-Packard to Google, and it remains one of the world’s great centres of learning and invention.

In the novel, Stanford stands at the far end of Asad’s journey: a world of ideas and ambition half a world away from the Khyber. It is also where Anna, a musician, enters his life, and where the two halves of his world begin to meet.

English translation

A Children’s playground, the world before me Nightly and daily, watching its pageantryThe throne of Suleiman; but a game to me Miracles of Messiah; a mere simplicityNo name I accept; less the critic of the world The existence of things, to me seems absurdIn its dust hides the desert, when I appear Rivers bow on their banks, in my fearDon’t ask how I feel when You are not around Sincerity betrays You, wherever I am foundCorrect are you, I’m as proud as can be My beloved’s reflection, like a mirror to meTo watch how flowers spread on my command A goblet of wine is all I demandJealous am I; ‘tis envy I feel His name, to me, it does not appealFaith restricts, while sin allures Ka’aba at my back; the church in the foreA lover am I; deceit is my charm Laila faults Majnoon, when in my armsTorture it is; but our union a delight The thought of the anguish of the separation nightMy wish: our separation be exciting and elate I know not what future holds from this dateMy hands may be numb, but my eyes still have light Leave the decanter and cup in my sightMy habits, my callings, he shares all with me Ghalib, I fancy; my confidant is he
