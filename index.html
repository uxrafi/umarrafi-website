A life across worlds.

Umar Rafi’s life has been an accidental journey across countries, cultures and professions. Born in Pakistan, he has lived in Iraq, USA, Canada, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Lebanon and Malaysia. His work in entrepreneurship, aviation, technology and finance, and the friendships he has made along the way, have shaped his curiosity about people and what drives them.

His studies and career led him to write books on technology and finance, but the writing closest to his heart is fiction. His novels draw on that learning and lived experience to follow characters across cultures and borders as they navigate love, loyalty and duty, exploring the choices that bring people together and the beliefs that set them apart.

Beyond the writing

Professional background

It began at the United States Air Force Academy, where I was the first Pakistani ever selected and one of only eight foreign cadets in my class, and then in the cockpit. In those years I learned that preparation is never wasted, that responsibility cannot be handed to someone else, and that every decision, however small, travels with you into the air. I still fly as a licensed pilot and coach skydivers, and few moments compare with the instant the door opens and the ground falls away. Up there, the sky is honest: it rewards discipline and punishes complacency. Those lessons still shape how I approach complex systems, with precision and an awareness of the people who depend on them.

From the cockpit I moved into the world where software meets finance, and stayed for more than twenty-five years. In that time I watched banking change three times: from systems that quietly recorded what institutions owed, to platforms that connected them with the people they serve, to intelligent agents now beginning to act on their behalf. I have been an engineer, an architect, a founder and a teacher along the way, and a student for rather longer than planned: a bachelor’s, three master’s and a doctorate later, I suspect I may never quite graduate. Every role has led me back to the same question: how should systems that hold people’s money and trust be built, and how should they behave?

In time those questions found their way into my writing and teaching. I explore how technology reshapes banking, from the way institutions manage risk and serve their customers to how financial systems endure shocks, and what they will need as intelligence moves closer to action: how authority is delegated, how decisions are evidenced, and how trust is kept when machines begin to act on our behalf.

The School of Athens

The artist

Raffaello Sanzio (1483–1520), known as Raphael, was born in Urbino, the son of Giovanni Santi, a painter and poet at the ducal court. Orphaned young, he trained in the circle of Perugino and by his early twenties was working in Florence, absorbing the lessons of Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo. He went on to become, with them, one of the three great masters of the High Renaissance, celebrated for the clarity, grace and harmony of his compositions.

His career was brilliant and brief. In Rome he painted for two popes, ran one of the largest workshops of his age and, after Bramante’s death, became chief architect of the new St Peter’s. He died in 1520, aged just thirty-seven, and was buried, at his own request, in the Pantheon.

Why he painted it

In 1508 Pope Julius II, unwilling to live in the rooms decorated for his predecessor and rival, Alexander VI, chose a new suite of apartments in the Vatican and summoned a group of painters to decorate them. Among them was Raphael, then about twenty-five and little known in Rome. He was given the room now called the Stanza della Segnatura, which most likely served as the pope’s private library. Across the corridor, Michelangelo was at work on the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel.

The room’s four walls follow the four branches of a Renaissance library: theology, philosophy, poetry and law. The School of Athens, painted between about 1509 and 1511, is philosophy’s wall, facing a fresco of the Church’s theologians across the room, so that human reason and divine revelation answer each other. Julius was so impressed that he dismissed the other painters, had some of their work painted over and gave Raphael the remaining rooms. The painting’s famous title is not Raphael’s own: it was given long after his death.

What it depicts

Around five metres high and almost eight metres wide, the fresco gathers more than fifty figures beneath a vast vaulted hall, inspired by Bramante’s designs for St Peter’s and watched over by statues of Apollo and Athena. At the centre walk Plato and Aristotle: Plato holds his Timaeus and points upwards to the world of ideas, while Aristotle holds his Ethics and stretches his hand over the earth. Around them, thinkers from different centuries debate, read, calculate and teach. Socrates counts off his arguments on his fingers, Pythagoras writes in a book, Diogenes sprawls on the steps, and Euclid bends over a slate with his compasses, while Ptolemy and Zoroaster hold globes of the earth and the heavens.

Raphael gave several of them the faces of his own contemporaries: Plato is often said to resemble Leonardo, Euclid bears Bramante’s features, and the brooding Heraclitus, absent from the preparatory drawing, has the likeness of Michelangelo, added after Raphael saw the first half of the Sistine ceiling. At the far right, Raphael painted himself among the crowd, looking out at us.

In spirit, the fresco is a cousin of Sadequain’s Treasures of Time on this site’s Writing page: both gather thinkers from different centuries into one imagined assembly, and both artists painted themselves into the crowd.

The full-size preparatory drawing, or cartoon, survives in the Pinacoteca Ambrosiana in Milan, one of the largest Renaissance drawings in existence. The fresco itself remains where Raphael painted it, now part of the Vatican Museums and seen by millions of visitors each year.

Understanding the numbers

Raphael left no key to his figures. The names come from tradition, beginning with Giorgio Vasari’s description of the fresco in 1550, and from centuries of later scholarship, which often disagree. Only a handful of identifications are secure.

21 figures are numbered here: 9 blue and 12 amber.

Blue: a widely accepted identification. These are the figures identified with the greatest confidence; the colour does not imply absolute historical certainty.

Amber: a proposed or disputed identification. A name or alternatives have been suggested, but the identity is not securely established; each description explains the uncertainty.

No number: no secure identification. Those figures remain unnamed here rather than being given a speculative identity.
